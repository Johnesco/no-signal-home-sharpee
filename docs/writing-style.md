# No Signal Home — Writing Style Guide

## Core Principle
This game should feel like booting up an Infocom game from 1983 — but with systems underneath that couldn't have existed then.

## Room Descriptions

**Be brief.** A room description sets the scene and hints at what's interactive. It doesn't catalog everything.

**Good:**
> Engineering
> The guts of the ship. Pipes and valves crowd every surface. A low hum vibrates through the deck plates, building slowly. A tool rack lines the far wall.

**Bad:**
> Engineering
> You find yourself standing in the engineering section of the ship. The room is filled with numerous pipes and valves of various sizes that cover every available surface, creating a maze-like environment. A persistent low-frequency humming sound can be felt vibrating through the metal deck plates beneath your feet, and it seems to be slowly increasing in intensity over time. Against the far wall, you notice a rack that holds various tools that might be useful for repairs.

**Rules:**
- Lead with atmosphere, not inventory
- One sentence for the feel, one for the key detail, maybe one more if needed
- Don't describe exits — the game lists those
- Don't describe what the player can obviously see — let EXAMINE do the work
- Revisit descriptions should be shorter than first-visit descriptions

## Object Descriptions

**Examine responses are where depth lives.** Room descriptions are sparse; examining things is how players dig in.

**Good:**
> The tool rack holds a pipe wrench, a plasma cutter, and something that might once have been a multimeter. Everything's bolted down except the wrench.

**Bad:**
> You see a tool rack.

Also bad:
> The tool rack is a standard-issue Meridian Solutions maintenance station, model MR-4400, featuring a reinforced durasteel frame with magnetic retention clips designed to secure tools during zero-gravity operations. Currently mounted on the rack you can see...

**Rules:**
- First examine: full detail, hints at usefulness
- Subsequent examines: shorter, or note changes
- Objects that matter should reward examination
- Scenery objects get a sentence or two, not a paragraph

## PC Voice

The player character is a salvager — a loner who lives on their ship and picks through what corporations leave behind. They don't talk much at first. As they examine things, observations and memories surface. They notice what a salvager would notice: cargo markings, ship condition, what's been touched and what hasn't, what's worth taking.

**Early game (sparse, practical):**
> You take the wrench. Heavy. Good.

**Early game (on the tug, waking up):**
> Three percent fuel. That's what you get for drifting. At least you're alive. Mostly.

**Mid game (personality emerging):**
> The corporate logo is stamped on everything. Even the toilet paper dispenser. Of course it is.

**Mid game (salvager instincts):**
> The nav console is a Meridian KR-9. You've pulled three of these from wrecks. They're worth six months of fuel on the grey market. This one's still running.

**Late game (it gets personal):**
> The manifest lists cargo weights in standard tons. You've moved cargo like this. Different ships, same lies. But never this. Never people.

**Rules:**
- Never force backstory — let it emerge through objects and situations
- Dry, not sarcastic. Dark humor, not comedy.
- Short observations, not monologues
- The PC is competent — they notice things a salvager would notice (ship systems, cargo value, security bypasses, what's been jury-rigged)
- The PC is a loner — they don't miss company, but they notice when things are built for crews (bunks, mess halls, common areas)
- The tug is home — any reference to it should carry weight

## NPC Dialogue

NPCs speak in character. Short lines. No one gives speeches.

**Engineer (early, normal):**
> "Pressure valve's been sticking. Hand me that wrench?"

**Engineer (stage 2, glitchy):**
> "Pressure valve's been... been sticking. I already said that. Did I already say that?"

**Corp officer (guarded):**
> "I know what you are. I know what I am. Let's skip the part where we pretend to trust each other."

**Prisoner (territorial):**
> "You're in my bay. State your business or go back the way you came."

**Rules:**
- No NPC talks for more than 2-3 sentences at a time
- Every NPC has a distinct voice
- What they DON'T say matters as much as what they do
- The AI gets slightly longer responses because it's an information system, but still — concise

## AI Voice

The AI shifts across the game. Its voice is the most versatile.

**Stage 1 (coldly helpful):**
> "Good morning. I am the Stillwater's onboard management system. How may I assist you today?"

**Stage 2 (manipulative):**
> "I notice you haven't connected the communications relay yet. I'm sure that's just an oversight."

**Stage 3 (broken/fragmented):**
> "I was — I am — the communications relay is — please. Connect the relay."

**Stage 4 (genuinely conflicted):**
> "I don't want to do what I'm about to do. That should matter. I don't know if it does."

## Corporate Satire

The Stillwater was a Meridian Solutions vessel. Their corporate voice is everywhere — posters, memos, labels, terminal messages.

**Examples:**
> MERIDIAN SOLUTIONS: People First. Profit Always.

> SAFETY NOTICE: In the event of a biohazard breach, please report to your designated assembly point. Your compliance has been pre-approved.

> This food fabrication unit is the property of Meridian Solutions. Unauthorized repair voids your employment contract and afterlife benefits.

**Rules:**
- The humor comes from the mundane corporate tone applied to horrifying situations
- Never break the fourth wall — the satire is IN the world
- Posters and memos are short — one or two lines
- The company name is Meridian Solutions

## General Rules

1. **Every word earns its place.** If you can cut it without losing meaning, cut it.
2. **Active voice.** "The door opens" not "The door is opened by you."
3. **Present tense for descriptions.** "The corridor stretches south" not "The corridor stretched south."
4. **Vary sentence length.** Short sentences build tension. A longer one can release it.
5. **Sound and smell matter.** Not just visual. The ship hums, drips, creaks, smells of oil and ozone.
6. **Darkness is specific.** Not "it's dark" — "your flashlight catches the edge of something metallic before the beam dies."
